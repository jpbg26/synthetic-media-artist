The Synthetic Media Artist (SMA): A Manifesto for Human Creativity in the Age of Generative Artificial Intelligence

By Justin S. Plevinski, Ph.D.

October 14, 2026

Purpose

This manifesto seeks to introduce and formally define the term Synthetic Media Artist (SMA), to establish the characteristics that distinguish a Synthetic Media Artist from a casual user of generative artificial intelligence, and to articulate the role of human creativity, intentionality, and artistic authorship within AI-assisted creative practice.

Drawing upon art history, aesthetics, philosophy of art, and scholarship on generative artificial intelligence, this manifesto argues that works produced through substantial human direction of generative AI can constitute legitimate artistic expression. Such works should not be dismissed categorically as artificial, derivative, or devoid of artistic merit simply because generative technologies participate in their production.

I am not arguing that every generated output is art. Access to a camera, synthesizer, or generative model gives a person a means of production. How they use it remains open to evaluation.

Photography and electronic music show that automation can become part of an artistic practice. Generative AI deserves the same opportunity for examination.

The question I want to examine is how human judgment shaped the work.

Central to this argument is the distinction among Synthetic Media Art, "AI slop," and "slopcore." Although all three may employ similar technologies, they differ in their creative intentions, processes, and degrees of human authorship.

Ultimately, this manifesto seeks to establish Synthetic Media Art as a legitimate contemporary artistic practice, and the Synthetic Media Artist as a recognizable creative practitioner within an evolving artistic landscape.

Defining the Synthetic Media Artist

A Synthetic Media Artist (SMA) is an individual who uses generative artificial intelligence as a central medium within an artistic workflow while maintaining substantial human direction, intentionality, and authorship. The SMA establishes the creative objective, directs the means of production, evaluates intermediate results, and determines the final artistic expression.

The artist takes an active role throughout development, evaluating what the system proposes and deciding what to retain, revise, or discard.

Artistic judgment is central to this definition. A lengthy prompt is not inherently more artistic than a short one. Likewise, generating hundreds of variations does not establish authorship if none of those variations were chosen for a reason.

This judgment may be expressed in many forms: writing, storyboarding, prompting, selection, compositing, performance, editing, arrangement, the design of a production workflow, or the framing of a single output within a concept that transforms its meaning. No single technique defines the Synthetic Media Artist. What is required is an intentional and identifiable human contribution to the artistic development of the work.

Within this framework, generative artificial intelligence is neither the sole artist nor merely an inert instrument. It is a technologically sophisticated medium capable of introducing unexpected possibilities, interpretations, and variations. Nevertheless, the human artist remains responsible for establishing the creative objectives, evaluating the results, and determining what is kept.

A Note on Terminology

The phrase "synthetic media" is frequently associated with deepfakes and other deceptive uses of generative technology. This manifesto adopts the term deliberately. The practice of the Synthetic Media Artist is, in an important sense, the opposite of deception: it is conducted openly, and its methods are disclosed rather than concealed.

What Constitutes Substantial Human Artistic Direction?

The term substantial is central to the definition of a Synthetic Media Artist, but it cannot be reduced to prompt length, hours spent, or number of revisions. It describes the significance of the human artistic contribution rather than its duration. The following dimensions provide a way to assess that contribution without imposing an arbitrary labor requirement.

Creative Investment: Time, Effort, and Iteration

The most readily observable dimension is the time and effort invested in a work. An SMA may spend hours, days, or longer constructing prompts, evaluating generations, correcting deficiencies, refining aesthetic choices, and integrating results into a finished composition.

Intentional iteration differs from arbitrary regeneration. The artist compares alternatives, identifies shortcomings, and revises instructions according to a creative objective. Each consequential revision expresses judgment about what the work should communicate.

Time invested is evidence of creative engagement, not proof of artistic authorship. Someone can regenerate images for hours without directing their aesthetic development; an experienced artist can realize a carefully conceived result in minutes.

Developed Artistic Capacity: The Knowledge Behind the Prompt

Substantiality also concerns the artistic knowledge, perceptual abilities, and creative judgment brought to the process. These capacities develop through study, observation, experimentation, practice, and individual creative aptitude.

An experienced photographer may understand how lighting, perspective, focal length, and composition affect emotion. A musician may recognize harmonic tension and structural weaknesses; a writer may understand pacing, symbolism, and characterization. When expressed in directions to a generative system, this knowledge can influence the work even when the prompt is brief.

Formal credentials are not required. Artistic knowledge may be self-taught, intuitive, interdisciplinary, or developed through sustained experimentation with generative tools. Experience alone, however, does not establish authorship: the artist must actually bring that capacity to bear on the particular work.

Efficient Artistic Direction: The Five-Minute Edge Case

Consider an experienced visual artist who spends five minutes writing a prompt specifying composition, lighting, emotional atmosphere, visual hierarchy, aesthetic references, and the relationships among subjects. The system produces a result matching the artist's vision on the first attempt, and the artist accepts it without iteration.

Such a work can exhibit substantial artistic direction. The brevity of execution does not erase the decisions made before the first word was entered. A concise prompt may concentrate years of artistic learning and an acquired ability to communicate a vision effectively to a model.

This is an edge case, not an automatic exemption for experienced creators. A skilled artist who enters a generic request and accepts an unanticipated result has not necessarily directed the outcome substantially. The question is whether their developed capacity was expressed in consequential creative decisions for this work.

Intentional Iteration: The Typical SMA Workflow

Although single-prompt authorship is possible, many complex SMA projects require sustained development because generative systems do not reliably realize an artistic vision on the first attempt.

The artist examines each result, identifies the gap between the output and the intended work, and decides how to close it. Refining visual composition, narrative continuity, characterization, musical arrangement, or the integration of separate assets becomes a process of artistic problem-solving.

Depending on the form and complexity, this work may take hours, days, or longer. These durations are illustrative, not minimum requirements.

Demonstrable Authorship: The Creative Record

Prompt histories, conversation transcripts, storyboards, reference images, intermediate generations, editing timelines, recordings, and project files can reveal the human decisions behind a finished work.

A record may show that the artist rejected technically impressive outputs because they failed to communicate the intended emotion, or that the artist repeatedly revised symbolism, composition, or narrative structure. Such evidence helps distinguish artistic direction from passive acceptance of machine outputs.

Documentation makes authorship more transparent and assessable, but it does not create authorship by itself. Long prompt histories can lack meaningful direction; short prompts can embody it. Nor must every artist maintain a complete record of their process.

Substantiality as a Multidimensional Judgment

Substantial human artistic direction is a qualitative judgment, not a numerical threshold. No minimum number of prompts, hours, revisions, or years of experience can reliably separate Synthetic Media Art from casual generative use.

Creative investment concerns time, effort, and deliberate refinement. Developed artistic capacity concerns knowledge, experience, perception, and judgment. Intentional artistic direction concerns how much of the work's concept, structure, style, and meaning the human determines. Creative agency concerns purposeful selection, rejection, modification, and integration. Demonstrable authorship concerns the evidence connecting those decisions to the result.

These dimensions need not be equally strong in every work. A novice may exercise substantial artistic direction, while an accomplished professional may produce a minimally directed output. The distinction is neither elitist nor based on credentials.

Ultimately, substantiality depends not on how long the human worked, but on how meaningfully the human's artistic vision and decisions shaped what the work became.

Art History and the Resistance to New Artistic Practices

The question of whether Synthetic Media Art constitutes "real art" is not without historical precedent. Throughout the history of artistic production, emerging styles, technologies, and creative practices have repeatedly challenged prevailing assumptions about artistic legitimacy.

This history does not establish that every rejected practice will eventually be recognized as valuable. It does, however, reveal the danger of treating familiar artistic methods as permanent prerequisites for artistic legitimacy.

Photography and the Question of the Machine

The nearest precedent to generative AI is photography. During the nineteenth century, critics frequently argued that photographs were produced by a machine rather than an artist, and therefore could not constitute art.

Walter Benjamin later argued that mechanical reproduction did not end art but transformed how art was understood. The photographer did not render the image by hand. Nevertheless, the photographer chose the subject, the framing, the moment, and the print. Over time, these choices came to be recognized as the substance of photographic authorship.1

Impressionism: Challenging the Standards of Artistic Legitimacy

During the nineteenth century, the Paris Salon, closely associated with the Académie des Beaux-Arts, favored technical precision, carefully modeled forms, polished surfaces, and subjects drawn from history, mythology, and religion.

Artists such as Claude Monet, Pierre-Auguste Renoir, Camille Pissarro, and Edgar Degas challenged these expectations. Their paintings emphasized fleeting effects of light, visible brushstrokes, unconventional compositions, and ordinary scenes of contemporary life.

The term Impressionism itself emerged from ridicule. In 1874, Louis Leroy, writing satirically in Le Charivari, drew upon the title of Monet's Impression, Sunrise (1872) to mock the perceived incompleteness of the exhibited works.2

The Impressionists were not incapable of painting according to academic conventions. Rather, they pursued different artistic objectives. Their work demonstrates an important principle: the standards used to judge an established tradition may be inadequate for evaluating an emerging one.

Fountain (1917)

In 1917, a commercially manufactured porcelain urinal, signed "R. Mutt" and titled Fountain, was submitted to an exhibition organized by the Society of Independent Artists in New York. Although the Society had promised to exhibit every work whose fee was paid, Fountain was quietly excluded from the exhibition.

Fountain is generally attributed to Marcel Duchamp. A proposed role for Elsa von Freytag-Loringhoven remains disputed; the surviving evidence does not establish her authorship.3

The artist had not sculpted the urinal or demonstrated conventional craftsmanship in its construction. Instead, the artist selected an existing object, repositioned it, assigned it a title, and presented it within an artistic context. The readymade thereby challenged the assumption that an artist must personally fabricate an object for that object to function as art.

Maurizio Cattelan and Comedian (2019)

More than a century later, Maurizio Cattelan exhibited Comedian, a banana attached to a wall with duct tape, at Art Basel Miami Beach in 2019. In 2024, an edition of the work sold at Sotheby's for approximately $6.2 million.

For supporters, Comedian demonstrated how artistic meaning can arise through context, humor, and the deliberate disruption of expectations. For critics, it exemplified the excesses of the contemporary art market. Both interpretations are relevant.

The controversy demonstrates that artistic legitimacy is neither universally agreed upon nor reducible to technical difficulty.

Implications for Synthetic Media Art

Arthur Danto examined how artistic theory and historical context allow an object to be understood as art.4 George Dickie developed an institutional account of art, emphasizing its place within the artworld.5

These accounts help explain why a single generated image may qualify as Synthetic Media Art when the artist’s selection and framing give it a specific meaning.

I include that possibility in this definition. A decisive act of selection can express authorship. Extended work often makes the artist’s decisions easier to trace, but a brief process can also carry a developed idea.

Distinguishing "AI Slop," Slopcore, and Synthetic Media Art

As generative artificial intelligence has become increasingly accessible, much of the content it produces has been dismissed under the pejorative label "AI slop." The term is often applied indiscriminately, describing virtually any content produced with generative tools regardless of its quality, originality, or degree of human involvement.

This broad application presents a fundamental problem: it conflates the technological means of production with the artistic merit of the resulting work. Three distinct terms are therefore required.

AI Slop

AI slop refers to generative content produced without meaningful artistic direction, in which the creative decisions that shape the work are left to the generative system rather than made by a human creator.

Appearance and production time provide limited evidence of this distinction. Some examples are visually appealing or amusing; others contain anatomical distortions, inconsistencies, or other recognizable artifacts. A work produced in minutes may reflect considerable artistic direction, while a work produced over many hours may reflect very little. In this manifesto, I use the term for a process in which the system supplies the substantive creative decisions and the user accepts them without purposeful evaluation.

Casual generation can be enjoyable. Entering a brief prompt that leaves the substantive creative decisions to the system, and sharing the result, is a form of casual creative participation, comparable to assembling a craft kit or following a painting tutorial. Such activities can be enjoyable and personally rewarding. They do not, however, demonstrate the intentional artistic authorship this manifesto associates with the Synthetic Media Artist.

Slopcore

Slopcore describes an emerging aesthetic sensibility that deliberately embraces the strange, exaggerated, incoherent, or visibly artificial characteristics of generative media. Within this aesthetic, distorted anatomy, impossible environments, and apparent generative errors become intentional stylistic devices rather than defects to be corrected.

Synthetic Media Art

Synthetic Media Art describes a mode of artistic practice characterized by substantial human direction, intentionality, and judgment.

The Relationship Among the Three

AI slop describes an absence of artistic direction; slopcore describes an aesthetic orientation; and Synthetic Media Art describes a practice. Because they describe different things, these categories are not mutually exclusive.

A Synthetic Media Artist may intentionally produce slopcore, exaggerating the absurdities of generative systems to create satire or surrealism. Conversely, a casual user may accidentally produce an image resembling the slopcore aesthetic without exercising meaningful artistic control.

Consequently, the distinction cannot be determined solely from the surface of a work. It rests upon how and why the work was created. As discussed below, this places a corresponding obligation on the Synthetic Media Artist to make that process visible.

The Synthetic Media Artist as Director and Producer

The emergence of generative artificial intelligence does not necessarily represent the disappearance of human artistic skill. It may instead represent a transformation in where that skill is exercised: from direct execution alone toward an expanded combination of conception, direction, selection, performance, composition, and refinement.

This form of skill is already recognized within established creative industries.

A film director may not possess the abilities of the actors, the cinematographer, the composer, or the visual effects specialists. Nevertheless, the director is frequently recognized as one of the principal authors of the finished film, a position articulated in film criticism as auteur theory. The director's contribution emerges through the development and communication of a creative vision, the evaluation of results, and the integration of many contributions into a unified work.6

Similarly, a music producer may not be the most accomplished musician involved in a recording. Yet the producer may exercise considerable influence over its arrangement, performance, sonic character, and emotional impact.

In both cases, artistic authorship is exercised through direction, judgment, and selection rather than exclusively through technical execution.

Authorship in Collaborative Writing

Written works can emerge from a division of creative labor. In collaborative textbook production, contributing authors draft material while a lead author or development editor reviews the manuscript for consistency, organization, and educational purpose. OpenStax describes this approach in its account of textbook development.7

Such arrangements show that a person can make a substantive contribution to a written work through its conception, structure, direction, and revision without personally composing every sentence. The writers’ contributions remain significant and deserve acknowledgment.

This offers a useful comparison for Synthetic Media Art. An artist may establish a detailed framework, evaluate proposed material, require changes, and integrate the results into a finished work. The strength of the claim to authorship depends on the substance of those interventions. An initial idea followed by uncritical approval provides much weaker evidence.

Human collaborators bring intentions and judgment of their own, so collaborative writing and generative production are not interchangeable. The narrower point is that authorship can include substantial direction and integration alongside direct execution.

The relationship between Socrates and Plato offers a related distinction between intellectual contribution and textual authorship. Socrates left no writings, yet remains recognized as a foundational philosopher through accounts produced by Plato, Xenophon, and others.8 His lack of personally written texts does not erase his intellectual contribution. Plato’s dialogues, however, are philosophical and literary works in their own right, and scholars continue to debate which ideas represent the historical Socrates and which reflect Plato’s thinking.9 This example illustrates how originating, developing, and recording ideas can involve different contributors. It supports recognizing intellectual contribution beyond the act of writing, while preserving the distinction between that contribution and authorship of a particular text.

Creative Direction as an Artistic Skill

The Synthetic Media Artist frequently occupies an analogous role. A generated scene may possess impressive visual quality while failing to communicate the intended emotion. A musical arrangement may be technically polished but stylistically inappropriate.

The SMA must recognize these deficiencies and determine how to correct them. This capacity to distinguish technically impressive output from artistically appropriate output is central to the practice.

Traditional knowledge of composition, color theory, harmony, narrative structure, or editing may strengthen this capacity. However, the absence of exceptional technical skill in a particular discipline does not negate an individual's capacity for artistic direction of the work as a whole.

Five Minutes of Execution, Thousands of Hours of Experience

A drawing completed in five minutes may draw on decades of practice. The same is possible with a prompt. Its immediate execution tells us little about the preparation that made it effective.

Consider two people who each spend five minutes writing instructions for an image model. A novice might describe the subject and leave the composition, lighting, and atmosphere to the system. An experienced artist might use those minutes to specify relationships among the subject, setting, perspective, light, and color because they know what each choice will contribute.

The experienced artist draws on visual knowledge as well as writing ability. Translating a visual intention into language takes precision and familiarity with how a particular system responds. A change in wording may matter because it alters the composition or emotional character of the result.

That ability develops through observation, experimentation, and learning the medium’s limits. Knowledge from photography, illustration, filmmaking, or other practices can inform it. Access to a model alone supplies none of that experience.

Formal training is not a prerequisite. A novice can arrive at a compelling work through intuition or an original concept. Experience can strengthen artistic decisions without guaranteeing a successful result.

For that reason, dismissing a work because its prompt took five minutes overlooks the knowledge behind it. The artist’s choices and their consequences deserve examination, even when the act of production was quick.

Artistic Decisions Cannot Be Counted by Counting Words

Ted Chiang’s 2024 essay “Why A.I. Isn’t Going to Make Art” raises a useful objection: a generative system supplies details that the person directing it has not specified. His approximation of a hundred-word prompt as roughly a hundred choices, however, provides an inadequate measure of human artistic contribution.10

A word is a unit of language, not a unit of artistic decision-making. A short instruction can express several related decisions. “Preserve the wrinkles” might communicate a commitment to the subject’s age, a rejection of idealized beauty, and a decision about how the portrait should represent its subject. Its significance depends on the artist’s purpose and how that instruction shapes the result.

An experienced artist may arrive at concise instructions after considering and rejecting many alternatives. Knowledge of composition, lighting, color, narrative, and the generative medium can make that language precise. The final prompt records only part of this deliberation. Counting its words cannot reconstruct the process behind it.

Photography offers a useful comparison. Pressing the shutter is one observable action, but the photograph may embody decisions about location, framing, timing, exposure, and the relationship between subject and surroundings. Similarly, submitting a prompt may conclude a substantial process of artistic preparation. Neither the click nor the submission reveals the full extent of that work.

This does not mean the artist chose every detail the system generated. An instruction can constrain a result while leaving considerable room for interpretation. A credible account of Synthetic Media Art should identify both the human contribution and the decisions delegated to the system.

Chiang’s concern about small-scale choices also deserves an answer. Those choices can profoundly affect a work. Synthetic Media Artists may engage them through targeted revisions, reference materials, selection, compositing, editing, and performance. A brief initial prompt therefore cannot stand in for an entire creative workflow.

The Limits of the Director Analogy

The director analogy has a limit.

Human collaborators possess their own intentions, experiences, and artistic agency. Generative systems may produce unexpected results that influence a project, but this does not establish that they possess humanlike intention or independent authorship.

The analogy remains strongest in the structure of the creative process: an individual establishes an artistic objective, directs the means of production, evaluates intermediate results, and assumes responsibility for the completed expression.

Making the Creative Process Visible: Documentation of Creative Labor

A finished work often conceals how it was made. Viewers may see a polished image or hear a complete song without knowing which decisions came from the artist and which were supplied by the system. An artist’s statement gives them something concrete to examine.

Synthetic Media Artists have a responsibility to accompany their work with a brief, honest account of its purpose, creative rationale, and workflow. The statement should identify the generative tools used and describe the artist’s contributions, such as drafting, performance, selection, compositing, editing, or refinement. It should explain how those choices served the work.

The account can be short. A caption may be enough for a simple piece; a complex project may benefit from examples of revisions or intermediate materials. Artists need not publish every prompt or disclose proprietary techniques. They should give audiences enough information to understand their role.

This documentation makes creative labor and skill visible, supports informed criticism, and helps others learn the methods of the medium. It can also reveal the experience behind a quick result. Hours worked and numbers of revisions are useful context, but they do not measure artistic value.

Concrete Examples: Distinguishing Synthetic Media Art from AI Slop

The distinction between Synthetic Media Art and AI slop becomes clearer when examining how individuals use generative artificial intelligence to produce similar types of media.

In the first four examples, both individuals work with the same subject and the same kind of generative system. Only the creative process differs. The final three examples address more difficult cases, since it is in these cases that the distinction is genuinely tested.

Example 1: Creating a Comic Book

Synthetic Media Artist (SMA)

An SMA sets out to create a four-panel comic about a man who discovers his girlfriend is cheating on him. Before using any generative system, the artist determines the joke: the man learns of the affair from his smart refrigerator.

The artist writes and revises the dialogue, develops a storyboard establishing panel arrangement, camera perspective, and pacing, and selects a visual style, such as the mid-century American newspaper strip.

The generative system is then used to produce panels according to that plan. Inconsistent character appearances, inappropriate facial expressions, and continuity errors are identified and corrected through additional prompting, selective regeneration, or manual editing. The artist completes the lettering and page layout.

AI Slop

A user enters a brief instruction such as: "Make a four-panel comic about a man who discovers his girlfriend is cheating on him. Make it funny."

The generative system independently determines the characters, dialogue, joke, composition, and style. The user publishes the initial output without meaningful revision.

Key distinction: The SMA writes the joke and directs its visual execution; the casual user delegates both to the generative system.

Example 2: Creating an Original Song Through Multi-Agent Creative Development

Synthetic Media Artist (SMA)

An SMA sets out to write a sad country song about a breakup. The artist begins with a specific narrative: a woman selling her former partner's truck discovers his handwriting on a note in the glovebox.

Rather than requesting a completed song, the artist designs a structured workflow of specialized AI agents. One agent develops the narrative, another drafts lyrics, another evaluates rhyme and meter, another advises on genre conventions, and another critiques each draft against criteria the artist has defined.

The artist establishes each agent's responsibilities and constraints, determines how work passes between them, and revises the workflow itself when it produces unsuitable results. Every draft returns to the artist, who rejects unsuitable suggestions, rewrites passages by hand, and restructures verses and choruses until the song expresses the intended meaning.

Only then does the artist develop a detailed musical direction and evaluate numerous generated arrangements.

AI Slop

A user enters a simple instruction such as: "Make a sad country song about a breakup." The generative system produces the lyrics, melody, arrangement, and vocal performance, and the user shares the result without substantial intervention.

Key distinction: Although the agents generate and critique material, the SMA designs the system, establishes its standards, and makes every final decision. The design of a creative production process is itself an authorial act, comparable to a producer assembling and directing a session band.

Example 3: Hybrid Music Production Using a Digital Audio Workstation

Synthetic Media Artist (SMA)

An SMA sets out to create an upbeat rock song with electric guitars and a catchy chorus. The artist develops a detailed prompt for a music-generation system such as Suno, evaluates multiple variations, and imports the most suitable backing track into a digital audio workstation such as Logic Pro.

There, the artist records lead vocals and harmonies, adds original guitar and keyboard performances, rearranges sections, replaces weak generated elements, and shapes the recording through equalization, compression, and automation.

AI Slop

A user enters the prompt "Create an upbeat rock song with electric guitars and a catchy chorus" and shares the completed recording without further development.

Key distinction: The SMA treats the generated material as a foundation for an original production; the casual user treats it as the finished product.

Example 4: Creating an Original Digital Composition

Synthetic Media Artist (SMA)

An SMA envisions a surrealist scene: a solitary human figure beneath an enormous mechanical tree in a decaying futuristic city.

Rather than generating the entire scene from a single prompt, the artist generates the mechanical tree, architectural ruins, sky, foreground vegetation, and human figure as separate assets. These are assembled in an image editor such as Adobe Photoshop, where the artist establishes perspective, scale, and focal point, paints light and shadow to unify the elements, and incorporates original photography or digital painting.

AI Slop

A user enters the prompt "Create a surreal futuristic city with a giant mechanical tree and a person standing underneath it." The generative system determines the composition, perspective, lighting, and color palette, and the user shares the initial output.

Key distinction: The SMA uses generative AI to produce raw materials and exercises direct compositional control; the casual user relies on the system to produce the completed composition.

Example 5: The Ambiguous Case of Extended Iteration

An individual generates fifty versions of a song from the same short prompt, adjusting stylistic parameters between attempts, and selects the forty-third result. The individual writes no lyrics and records no performances.

Whether this constitutes Synthetic Media Art depends on whether the iteration was guided by intention. If the individual knew what qualities they were seeking and can articulate why the selected version succeeded where the others failed, the process reflects selection informed by artistic judgment, a modest but genuine form of authorship. If the individual simply regenerated until something sounded acceptable, the process more closely resembles chance than direction.

The number of iterations does not settle the question. The process statement is where the difference becomes visible.

Example 6: The Conceptual Case

An artist instructs an image-generation model to produce "the most average painting possible," accepts the first output without modification, and exhibits it under the title Median, accompanied by the full prompt and a statement concerning the tendency of statistical models to flatten aesthetic taste.

This work qualifies as Synthetic Media Art. The technical labor involved was minimal. The artistic judgment was not: the decision to leave the image unaltered is the meaning of the work. As with Fountain, the authorial contribution resides in concept and context.

Example 7: Intentional Slopcore

An artist produces a series of short parody advertisements for wellness products. Rather than correcting the failures of the generative system, the artist deliberately provokes them: extra fingers grasping a supplement bottle, smiles that dissolve mid-sentence, product labels rendered in near-English. From hundreds of generated clips, the artist selects those whose errors best serve the satire and edits them to the rhythm of original advertising copy the artist has written.

The resulting work resembles the most extreme examples of AI slop. Nevertheless, it constitutes Synthetic Media Art, because its failures were chosen, timed, and directed toward an expressive purpose. Its aesthetic is slopcore; its practice is authorship.

The Continuum of Human Artistic Involvement

These examples illustrate different degrees of creative participation, but they should not be interpreted as rigid categories. The number of prompts, the length of instructions, the quantity of iterations, or the complexity of the software used should not independently determine whether someone qualifies as a Synthetic Media Artist.

AI slop typically prioritizes obtaining an output. Synthetic Media Art prioritizes realizing an artistic vision. The former treats generation as the completion of the creative process; the latter treats generation as one stage within a broader process of artistic development.

The following principles summarize the practice of the Synthetic Media Artist. They are not intended as a mechanical scoring system. A Synthetic Media Artist may demonstrate substantial authorship through only some of them.

Principles of the Synthetic Media Artist

  1. Creative conception. Develop an artistic objective or concept that gives the work direction.
  2. Human direction. Shape the content, structure, style, and expressive character of the work, and decide which generated proposals to accept.
  3. Intentional iteration. Guide revisions with artistic judgment and a reason for each change.
  4. Creative contribution. Contribute through writing, performance, composition, design, editing, or the design of the production process.
  5. Artistic integration. Combine and refine the materials into a coherent finished work.
  6. Aesthetic freedom. Use error, distortion, and artificiality when they serve the artistic intention.
  7. Final authorship. Accept responsibility for the finished work and its relationship to the work of other creators.
  8. Transparency. Provide the artist’s statement described in Documentation of Creative Labor.

Aesthetic and Social Possibilities of Synthetic Media Art

The principles above concern the artist’s practice. The following discussion considers what the medium makes possible, from combining forms to widening access and creating responsive works.

Aesthetic Independence and Multimodal Expression

Synthetic Media Art is not intended to replace or merely copy established art forms. It is an expressive practice in its own right. Generative models learn patterns and relationships from broad training materials, enabling artists to combine and transform conventions across genres, periods, and media. These possibilities are not wholly unprecedented in art history, but their fluid availability changes the scope of artistic experimentation.

Visual artists can relocate subjects into unexpected environments and activities, explore impossible spatial relationships, or embrace absurdity, surrealism, and the peculiarities of generative representation. Works may range from photorealism to extreme abstraction. Rather than concealing the medium's characteristics, artists may use them to realize a distinctive vision.

In music, an artist can combine genres, historical eras, vocal identities, instrumental arrangements, and textures in ways that might be impractical to produce with a conventional studio ensemble. In writing, an author can transform an original draft through different styles, perspectives, registers, and tones, while using language models for editorial feedback, story organization, and continuity assistance. In film and animation, artists can explore impossible environments, temporal transformations, and novel combinations of cinematic languages.

Aesthetic independence does not demand a recognizable synthetic look: the medium can engage established traditions without being reducible to imitation.

Human Intention and Discovery

Human artistic intention remains at the forefront of Synthetic Media Art. An artist may begin with a highly developed vision or discover a compelling possibility through experimentation and serendipity. Both can involve genuine authorship when the artist exercises meaningful creative judgment through conception, direction, selection, revision, contextualization, or presentation.

The artist need not control every generated detail, nor does the number of prompts or hours worked measure artistic value. What matters is the significance of human decisions in shaping the expressive work. Chance and unexpected results can become artistic materials when recognized and purposefully developed.

The Democratization of Creation

Synthetic Media Art can reduce technical, financial, physical, educational, and logistical barriers between imagination and artistic realization. A person without a film crew may explore cinematic ideas; a composer without an orchestra may develop complex arrangements; a writer may obtain structural and editorial assistance; and a visually imaginative creator may work without mastering every conventional method of manual execution.

Wider access creates opportunities to practice and experiment. It does not guarantee achievement. Artistic judgment and knowledge of form still matter, and generative systems carry costs and limitations of their own.

Expressive Capability: Participatory Generativity

Participatory Generativity is an optional capability, not a defining requirement of Synthetic Media Art. Through APIs, locally deployed models, or interactive software, an artist can create a responsive work that adapts to a consumer's choices, identity, preferences, or needs. Static paintings, recorded songs, completed films, and fixed novels remain fully compatible with Synthetic Media Art.

An artist might build a fictional universe whose structure, themes, and rules remain consistent while consumers define their own characters and influence story outcomes. Another might create a three-dimensional historical time capsule whose perspectives and environments adapt to a participant's chosen background or interests, distinguishing documented history from imaginative reconstruction. A writer might leave a story's setting, clothing, or character presentation variable while preserving its central conflict and emotional arc. A literary experience might adapt reading difficulty, vocabulary, language, or narrative voice to a reader's needs.

Such works distinguish artistic invariants—the themes, structures, rules, or aesthetic commitments preserved by the creator—from participatory variables—the aspects the audience may influence. This artist-directed variability can produce individualized experiences without erasing the work's creative identity. Participants should retain meaningful choice over personal information they share; demographic categories should not rigidly determine their experience.

The artist decides which parts of the work remain fixed and which the participant can change. That boundary is itself a creative decision.

Copyright and Human Authorship: A Legal Distinction

Artistic legitimacy and legal copyrightability are not synonymous. The U.S. Copyright Office's 2023 registration guidance requires applicants to identify human-authored elements and disclose more-than-de-minimis AI-generated material.11 Its January 2025 report explains that sufficiently expressive human selection, arrangement, or modification can support protection, while merely supplying prompts does not, by itself, establish copyrightable authorship.12 These administrative positions concern U.S. copyright law and should not be mistaken for a universal definition of art.

The use of copyrighted material in AI training presents a separate and contested legal and policy issue, discussed in the Copyright Office's 2025 pre-publication training report.13

Conclusion

I propose Synthetic Media Artist as a name for a practice in which generative systems play a substantial role and human judgment shapes the result. That judgment may appear in a storyboard, a recorded performance, a carefully composed prompt, an edit, or a decision about how to present the work.

The examples in this manifesto offer ways to examine that contribution. An honest account of the process makes the argument open to criticism: audiences can consider what the artist intended, what they did, and whether the finished work carries that intention.
